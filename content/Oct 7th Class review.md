# Polyhedron, Convex Hull, Polytope & Convex Combination

## 1. Big Picture

Today I learned several concepts that feel very similar to linear algebra:

- linear combination → convex combination
- span → convex hull
- basis vectors → vertices / extreme points

The main difference is that convex combinations have restrictions on the coefficients.

---

## 2. Linear Combination vs Convex Combination

### Linear Combination

A linear combination has the form

$$
x = \lambda_1 v_1 + \lambda_2 v_2 + \cdots + \lambda_k v_k
$$

where

$$
\lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}
$$

The coefficients can be positive, negative, or larger than 1.

So linear combinations can extend infinitely.

Example:

$$
v_1=(1,0), \quad v_2=(0,1)
$$

Then

$$
\operatorname{span}\{v_1,v_2\}=\mathbb{R}^2
$$

---

### Convex Combination

A convex combination looks almost the same:

$$
x = \lambda_1 v_1 + \lambda_2 v_2 + \cdots + \lambda_k v_k
$$

but now we require

$$
\lambda_i \ge 0
$$

and

$$
\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\cdots+\lambda_k=1.
$$

So a convex combination is basically a **weighted average**.

> [!idea]
> Linear combination = arbitrary weights  
> Convex combination = nonnegative weights that sum to 1

---

## 3. Convex Hull

The convex hull of some points is the set of all convex combinations of those points.

For points

$$
v_1,\dots,v_k
$$

we write

$$
\operatorname{conv}\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}.
$$

### Geometric intuition

- 2 points → line segment
- 3 non-collinear points → triangle
- more points → polygon / higher-dimensional convex shape

Example:

$$
A=(0,0), \quad B=(4,0)
$$

Then

$$
x=\lambda A+(1-\lambda)B,
\quad 0\le \lambda \le 1
$$

produces every point on the line segment between $A$ and $B$.

> [!important]
> Span can extend infinitely.
> Convex hull stays inside the region formed by the generating points.

---

## 4. Polyhedron

A polyhedron is a set defined by linear inequalities.

$$
P=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n: Ax\le b\}.
$$

In linear optimization, the feasible region is usually a polyhedron.

Example:

$$
x\ge0,\quad y\ge0,\quad x+y\le4.
$$

This creates a triangular feasible region.

---

## 5. Polytope

A polytope is a bounded polyhedron.

So:

$$
\boxed{\text{polytope = bounded polyhedron}}
$$

Example:

$$
0\le x\le1,\quad 0\le y\le1
$$

is a square, so it is a polytope.

But

$$
x\ge0,\quad y\ge0
$$

is unbounded, so it is a polyhedron but not a polytope.

---

## 6. Connection Between Polytope and Convex Hull

A polytope can be described by its vertices.

For example, if

$$
P=\operatorname{conv}\{v_1,\dots,v_k\},
$$

then every point inside $P$ can be written as

$$
x=\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i v_i
$$

where

$$
\lambda_i\ge0,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_i=1.
$$

So a polytope is generated by convex combinations of its vertices.

---

## 7. Connection to Linear Algebra

| Linear Algebra | Convex Geometry / Optimization |
|---|---|
| Linear combination | Convex combination |
| Span | Convex hull |
| Basis vectors | Vertices / extreme points |

But this analogy is not exact.

A basis must be linearly independent.

Vertices do not need to be linearly independent.

For example, a square in $\mathbb{R}^2$ has 4 vertices, but a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$ only has 2 vectors.

> [!note]
> Basis vectors generate a vector space using linear combinations.  
> Vertices generate a polytope using convex combinations.

---

## 8. Why This Matters in Linear Optimization

A linear program has the form

$$
\max c^Tx
$$

subject to

$$
Ax\le b.
$$

The constraints create a polyhedron.

If the feasible region is bounded, it is a polytope.

In many linear programming problems, an optimal solution can be found at a vertex / extreme point of the feasible region.

This explains why graphical LP problems often focus on checking corner points.

---

## 9. One-Sentence Summary

> Linear combinations generate vector spaces, while convex combinations generate convex shapes.

And in linear optimization:

$$
\text{linear inequalities}
\rightarrow
\text{polyhedron}
\rightarrow
\text{polytope if bounded}
\rightarrow
\text{convex hull of vertices}
$$
